The wrinkle in the prior-art finding

The frameworks that found "turn-based is wrong" were solving either an AI agent deciding when to reply, or true simultaneous conference interpretation of one continuous speaker to a passive audience. Boostlingo's actual product is neither — it's interpretation for a two-party conversation, and the brief's own stability test says "sustain a 5-minute back-and-forth conversation." Two humans taking turns is inherently utterance-segmented even with a human interpreter in the loop. That's why this isn't a straight adoption of the prior-art conclusion — it's a genuine open question for this product.

Genuinely an upgrade layered on Option 2, not a replacement for it — Deepgram's speech_final/UtteranceEnd stay as the guaranteed fallback boundary, so worst case this degrades to Option 2's behavior. Closest prior-art parallel: Pipecat's two-stage VAD → "Smart Turn" design (cheap acoustic gate, then a semantic-completeness check) — same idea, but the completeness check runs on the transcript text via an LLM here rather than on raw audio via a purpose-trained model.

Real risk: an LLM call in the hot path on every partial-transcript update adds latency/cost and a new failure mode — needs the VAD fallback to be robust, and the completeness-check prompt tuned to avoid chopping sentences awkwardly (false positive) or never firing early (false negative, no benefit over Option 2 alone).

Which feels more organic to the listener? answering your question — my opinion

My honest read: the hybrid is more likely to feel natural in this system, even though continuous is the theoretically "more simultaneous" ideal.

Why continuous doesn't automatically win on "organic"

  • True simultaneous interpretation means the translated voice plays while the source speaker is still talking — that's how skilled human conference interpreters work, but it means two voices overlapping, which sounds chaotic to a listener who isn't used to it.
  • The polished examples (LiveKit's gemini-live-translate, gpt-realtime-translate) feel smooth because the underlying model does its own sophisticated internal segmentation — that smoothness is bought with heavy model training, not just "no discrete boundary."
  • A DIY rolling-window implementation, built in this timeframe without that training, risks cutting mid-clause or mid-word — which reads as more jarring than a clean pause, not less.

Why the hybrid fits this product specifically

  • It produces linguistically complete, grammatically clean translated sentences — proper prosody, no truncation — because the LLM only cuts at real clause boundaries.
  • Boostlingo's actual product is a two-party conversation (healthcare/legal/business calls), not a conference broadcast. That's consecutive interpretation territory: a real human consecutive interpreter also pauses briefly, then delivers one clean sentence at a time — listeners in this domain already expect that cadence.
  • The brief, cheap pauses from clause-level cutting are short enough not to feel like "waiting," but long enough to avoid the overlapping-voices problem entirely.

Caveat: this is a judgment call, not a measured fact — worth actually listening to both once built, and it's exactly the kind of thing the brief's subjective-quality comparison write-up should capture with real reactions, not just this reasoning.

Does listening stop while the LLM judges? answering your question

No. Audio capture and Deepgram STT never pause for anything. The LLM check runs as an async side-channel on a snapshot of the transcript-so-far — it doesn't gate the microphone, the WebSocket, or Deepgram in any way. The speaker can keep talking indefinitely while a check is in flight; new words just keep appending to the buffer in parallel. 
sequenceDiagram
    participant S as Speaker
    participant DG as Deepgram STT
    participant BE as Backend buffer
    participant LLM as LLM clause-check
    participant TR as Translation + TTS

    S->>DG: continuous audio (never pauses)
    loop as speech continues
        DG->>BE: is_final segment (words)
        BE->>BE: append to running transcript
    end
    Note over BE,LLM: debounced trigger — at most one check in flight per speaker
    BE->>LLM: snapshot of transcript up to word N (async, non-blocking)
    par LLM judging (~100-300ms)
        LLM-->>BE: verdict: complete / not complete
    and speaker keeps talking, completely unaffected
        S->>DG: more audio, no pause
        DG->>BE: more is_final segments appended past word N
    end
    alt LLM says complete (as of word N)
        BE->>TR: translate words[0..N] only; words after N start the next window
    else LLM says not complete, or a check is already running
        Note over BE: no early cut — just keep accumulating
    end
    par Independent fallback, always running
        DG->>BE: speech_final / UtteranceEnd fires on its own timer
        BE->>TR: translate whatever has accumulated (Option 2's behavior)
    end
  • Throttling: a check doesn't fire on every single word — it's debounced (e.g. on new final segments, or candidate boundaries like clause-ending punctuation) and only one check is in flight per speaker at a time, so checks don't pile up faster than the LLM can answer.
  • The race is resolved by position, not by locking: a "complete" verdict only ever applies to the exact word range it was asked about (word 0..N). Anything the speaker said after the snapshot was taken simply carries forward into the next window — nothing is dropped, re-checked awkwardly, or blocked.
  • Deepgram's own endpointing keeps running independently the whole time, as a completely separate signal — whichever of the two (LLM verdict or speech_final) fires first for a given stretch of speech wins and triggers the cut.

How do partial transcripts feed the live display?

The brief requires live transcripts showing both source and target text "as they're produced" (success criterion 6). With segmentation now decided, here's my recommendation for both sides of the display:

Source-language transcript

Stream Deepgram's is_final segments straight to the UI as they arrive — words appear and settle in near-real-time, independent of the segmentation decision entirely. This is the simplest possible design and matches how live captioning normally works.

Target-language transcript

Appears in chunks aligned to each translated segment (from decision 1) — but stream the LLM's translation response token-by-token as it generates, rather than waiting for the full segment translation to complete. Reduces perceived latency of the text appearing, even though TTS audio will lag slightly behind it.

Net effect: the source pane feels like live captioning (continuous), the target pane fills in segment-by-segment but with each segment itself streaming in — not a single delayed block of text.
✓ Decided — Partial transcript display: source pane streams Deepgram output directly; target pane fills per-segment, streaming token-by-token within each segment.

Target-text chunking to TTS — word-by-word or sentence-level?

Pipecat names this the SENTENCE-vs-TOKEN tradeoff: SENTENCE mode buffers to a full sentence (~200-300ms added latency, better prosody); TOKEN mode streams tokens straight to TTS as they arrive (lower latency, quality depends on the TTS provider's own handling of partial text).

Recommendation: TOKEN mode, with an explicit flush at each segment boundary

ElevenLabs' streaming endpoint is built for this — it accepts incremental SendText messages and buffers internally via chunk_length_schedule, so it already handles natural-sounding grouping without the caller needing to pre-batch into sentences. And since segmentation (decision 1) already cuts at clause boundaries — not full multi-sentence turns — the segments arriving here are already TTS-appropriately sized. Streaming translation tokens straight through, then calling flush: true exactly when a segment completes, gets low latency and reasonable prosody without adding a second buffering layer on top of one that's already there.

Browser↔backend protocol, and the last small pick: translation provider

Clarifying your question — ElevenLabs does NOT do the translation here. In this pipeline: Deepgram transcribes (speech→source text), the translation LLM translates (source text→target text), and ElevenLabs only converts already-translated text into speech (target text→target audio) — see the "Translation LLM" and "ElevenLabs TTS" boxes as two separate stages in the pipeline diagram above. The reasonable source of confusion: ElevenLabs sells a separate "Dubbing" product that bundles translation + speech generation into one call. We're deliberately not using that — we're using ElevenLabs' plain streaming TTS endpoint (stream-input), which only speaks text it's given, in whatever language that text is in. Using the bundled Dubbing product instead would collapse two of our three provider-swap points into one vendor, working against the brief's "provider abstractions... swapping a provider should be a contained change" requirement, and it isn't built for our low-latency incremental streaming shape the way the plain TTS endpoint is.
Follow-up — is Dubbing higher-latency than LLM+TTS? Almost certainly yes, by a wide margin — but flagging honestly: this specific claim was not part of ticket 04's verified primary-source research (that research was scoped to the plain streaming TTS endpoint only). My understanding, unverified here: ElevenLabs' Dubbing product is built as an asynchronous whole-file workflow — submit a video/audio file, wait, get a dubbed file back — which puts it in a fundamentally different latency class (plausibly seconds-to-minutes for a full clip) than a per-utterance streaming target under 2s. It doesn't change the recommendation either way, since Dubbing was already ruled out above for the provider-abstraction reason regardless of its latency — but if this specific number ever mattered for the write-up, it'd be worth a dedicated primary-source check rather than relying on this.

One full-duplex WebSocket per Cascade session, carrying both binary audio frames and JSON control/event messages — mirrors Pipecat's RTVI envelope pattern from the prior-art research, adapted to what this pipeline actually needs.

Is WebSocket the only option? No — the main alternative is WebRTC (audio track + data channel), which is what Realtime mode (ticket 03) uses for its direct OpenAI connection. Not recommending it here because: both providers we're bridging to, Deepgram and ElevenLabs, already speak WebSocket themselves (per ticket 04's research) — so a WebRTC browser↔backend leg would need its own media server/transcoding step just to convert back into WebSocket-framed audio before reaching either provider, adding real infrastructure (e.g. an aiortc-based server) for no latency or quality benefit. Plain WebSocket is also literally what Pipecat's own non-hosted-SFU transport module uses for this exact shape (ticket 12's research). If mode-transport symmetry with Realtime mode mattered more than build time, WebRTC would be the honest alternative to weigh — flagging it rather than deciding it away silently.

Binary audio frames aren't taggable inline, so a small JSON "meta" message immediately precedes each one — safe because WebSocket preserves message ordering on a single connection. segmentId threads every message (transcripts, boundary events, audio, latency) so the client can correlate everything belonging to one translated unit. latency messages feed forward directly into ticket 08. error messages feed forward into ticket 10.

translation provider — the other open item

Recommendation: OpenAI

Ticket 04's research called OpenAI vs. Anthropic "close to a toss-up" on latency/quality for streaming translation — no strong differentiator either way. The tie-breaker: OpenAI is already a required dependency for Realtime mode (gpt-realtime), so using it for Cascade-mode translation too means one fewer distinct provider/API key to manage within the time budget. The provider abstraction (ticket 06) keeps this swappable if the comparison write-up later shows Anthropic is meaningfully better.
